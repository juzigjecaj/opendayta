PII Transformation for Enterprise Datasets

PII Transformation for Enterprise Datasets

Last updated October 23, 2026

Introduction

Enterprise data contains information about how businesses actually operate. It includes internal communications, engineering documentation, financial records, project histories, employee workflows, and operational processes. This information is particularly valuable for training AI models because it captures real-world decisions, relationships, and sequences of work that are difficult to reproduce using publicly available data.


However, these datasets often contain personally identifiable information (PII), including employee names, email addresses, phone numbers, usernames, financial identifiers, and other information that can reveal someone's identity.


Simply removing or masking these details creates another problem. It can remove important context, break connections between records, and make the resulting dataset less useful for training.


For example, an employee might appear across hundreds of documents under their full name, email address, username, and employee ID. If each occurrence is independently replaced or removed, the resulting dataset may no longer preserve the relationships between those records.


To address this, we developed flow-transform 1.0, a framework that replaces real-world identities with consistent synthetic identities across an entire enterprise dataset.

Instead of treating every document independently, the system processes the entire collection as a connected dataset. It identifies sensitive information, determines which references belong to the same person or entity, generates corresponding synthetic identities, and applies those identities consistently across all relevant files.


The objective is to remove the connection to real-world identities without losing the relationships, context, and information that make the original data valuable.

This article explains how the transformation process works, how its quality is evaluated, and what the results demonstrate.

1. How Enterprise Data Transformation Works

1.1 Why individual redaction is insufficient


Traditional anonymization systems generally focus on identifying sensitive information and removing or replacing it.

This works for isolated pieces of information, but enterprise datasets are more complicated.

Consider an employee named Sarah Mitchell who appears in different systems:

  • HR records: Sarah Mitchell

  • Email communications: sarah.mitchell@company.com

  • Internal chat: @smitchell

  • Project documentation: Employee ID 48291

  • Financial records: Approver Sarah M.


These references may all describe the same individual.


If each file is processed independently, the system might replace the name with one synthetic person, the email with an unrelated address, and the username with another identity.

Although the original information has been removed, the relationships between records have also been lost.


Our approach addresses this by identifying the underlying entity first and then generating one synthetic identity that is reused everywhere that entity appears.

For example:


Original informationTransformed informationSarah MitchellEmily Cartersarah.mitchell@company.comemily.carter@example.com@smitchell@ecarterEmployee ID 48291Employee ID 73516Sarah M.Emily C.

The replacement values differ depending on the original field, but they all refer to the same synthetic person.


The same principle applies to companies, accounts, projects, addresses, and other protected entities.


1.2 The transformation process

The system follows four primary stages:

  1. Data inventory and segmentation

  2. PII detection and entity resolution

  3. Synthetic identity generation and transformation

  4. File reconstruction and validation


An additional AI review layer can operate between detection and transformation to resolve difficult cases and improve accuracy.


Stage 1: Data inventory and segmentation


The process begins by ingesting the eligible enterprise dataset.


The system identifies each incoming file, determines its format, records relevant metadata, and prepares it for processing.


Depending on the source, the dataset can contain documents, spreadsheets, database records, messages, images, audio, video, and other business artifacts.


Large files are divided into smaller segments so they can be processed efficiently without losing their connection to the original artifact.


During ingestion, the system records information needed to reconstruct the original files later.

This includes details such as:


  • Original file identifiers and locations

  • File formats and metadata

  • Document structure and page information

  • Spreadsheet sheets, rows, and columns

  • Message and conversation boundaries

  • Archive contents and other structural information


An inventory of all eligible inputs is also created. This establishes exactly which files are expected to be processed, allowing the system to identify missing, rejected, or incomplete outputs later.


Stage 2: PII detection and entity resolution


Once the dataset has been inventoried and segmented, specialized detectors and models scan the content for personally identifiable information.


The system looks for different categories of sensitive information, including:


  • Personal names

  • Email addresses

  • Phone numbers

  • Physical addresses

  • Employee and government identifiers

  • Financial account information

  • Usernames and account identifiers

  • Other values that can identify an individual directly or indirectly


Detection is not limited to obvious patterns.


Enterprise data frequently contains misspellings, inconsistent formatting, abbreviations, OCR errors, unusual separators, and references that only become identifiable when interpreted in context.


For example, a person's name might appear as:


  • Sarah Mitchell

  • Sarah M.

  • S. Mitchell

  • @smitchell

  • sarah.mitchell


The system must determine whether these references belong to the same underlying person.

This is where entity resolution becomes important.


Instead of treating every detected value as a separate piece of PII, the system links related mentions across the entire dataset.


A shared identity store maintains information about each identified entity, including:


  • Its canonical identity

  • Known names and aliases

  • Associated PII categories

  • Context surrounding individual references

  • Confidence levels for identity matches


This allows different processing workers to reference the same identity information rather than independently generating replacements.


The distinction is important: detecting a sensitive value tells the system that something needs to be transformed. Entity resolution determines which real-world identity that value belongs to.


Stage 3: Synthetic identity generation


After identifying and connecting the references belonging to each protected entity, the system creates a synthetic identity.


Rather than generating replacements independently for every field, it creates a coordinated identity bundle.


Depending on the information available and the transformation policy, this bundle can include:


  • Full name

  • First and last name

  • Email address

  • Username

  • Employer

  • Physical address

  • Employee identifier

  • Other permitted attributes


The generated identity is designed to maintain consistency between related attributes.

For example, if the original person is Sarah Mitchell, the system might generate Emily Carter as the replacement identity.


It can then generate an associated email address, username, and employee identifier that all belong to Emily Carter.


The mapping between the original entity and its synthetic counterpart remains fixed throughout the processing run.


This means that if Sarah appears in 500 different files, every occurrence is transformed using the same synthetic identity.


The system also ensures that different source identities remain distinct. Two employees should not accidentally receive the same synthetic identity, as this would create false relationships between records.

Stage 4: Applying transformations across the dataset

Once synthetic identities have been generated, the system applies the corresponding replacements to every identified occurrence.


The transformation depends on the original context.

For example, a full name might be replaced with another full name, while an email address is replaced with an email address belonging to the same synthetic person.


A username is replaced with a corresponding synthetic username, and employee identifiers are replaced with new identifiers.


The system also preserves relationships between entities.


If one employee reports to another, the transformed dataset should retain that reporting relationship, even though both identities have changed.


Similarly, if an employee is assigned to a project, appears in an email thread, and approves a financial transaction, those connections remain intact after transformation.

The objective is not to preserve the original identifying values. It is to preserve the useful relationships between records after the identities have been changed.


Stage 5: Reconstructing and validating the files

After the transformations are complete, the processed segments are reassembled into their original artifacts.


The system uses the metadata collected during ingestion to reconstruct the files while preserving their important structural properties.


Depending on the file format, validation can include checking:


  • Spreadsheet rows, columns, formulas, and sheet names

  • Document page counts and layout

  • Message and conversation structure

  • Subtitle timing information

  • Archive members and file organization

  • Content outside the intended replacement regions


This stage checks that the transformed files remain usable and that the expected outputs have actually been generated.


Structural validation is separate from identity validation. A document can open correctly while still containing an incorrect replacement or an exposed real identity.

For this reason, both types of checks are necessary.

2. AI-Assisted Review and Human Validation

2.1 Agentic review


Automated detection systems can identify many instances of PII, but difficult cases require additional reasoning.


For example, a person's name might be misspelled, partially obscured, split across multiple lines, or referenced through an alias that only becomes recognizable when compared with other documents.


To handle these situations, flow-transform 1.0 includes an optional agentic review layer.

An AI agent examines detected candidates alongside their surrounding context and the shared identity store.


Rather than simply accepting or rejecting individual detection results, it can perform several types of actions:


  • Confirm whether a detected value is actually PII.

  • Reject values incorrectly identified as sensitive.

  • Determine whether different aliases belong to the same person.

  • Separate identities that have incorrectly been merged.

  • Resolve misspelled or degraded references using surrounding context.

  • Identify additional PII that was missed during the initial detection stage.

  • Apply corrections across related documents.


For example, if the system encounters Sarah Mitchell in one document and S. Mitchell in another, the agent can examine the surrounding information and determine whether both references describe the same employee.


If they do, the references are connected to the same synthetic identity.


If two similar names actually belong to different people, the agent can separate them to avoid creating an incorrect connection.


The agent operates with access to cross-document context, allowing it to resolve cases that cannot reliably be handled by examining individual text spans.


However, broader agent access also introduces additional complexity. Ambiguous references can sometimes be incorrectly connected, creating identity collisions.


For this reason, we focus the agentic layer primarily on adjudication and additional review rather than allowing unrestricted modifications throughout the dataset.


The benchmark evaluates the pipeline both with and without this layer to measure its actual contribution.


2.2 Human review


Human reviewers are involved in several parts of the evaluation and quality-control process.

Their role is not to manually inspect every record. Instead, they establish the evaluation criteria, review representative samples, identify recurring errors, and assess cases that require contextual judgment.


The process includes four main activities.


Establishing the PII taxonomy


Reviewers define the categories of information that should be protected and provide canonical examples.


They also help establish the expected relationships between known entities and their transformed counterparts.


Identifying recurring failures


Reviewers examine transformation errors and look for patterns that appear across multiple files.


For example, they might identify a specific region of financial documents where bank account information repeatedly remains exposed.


Evaluating contextual quality


Some properties cannot be reliably evaluated through automated rules alone.

Reviewers assess whether replacements make sense in context, whether the transformed information remains understandable, and whether the overall presentation resembles the original.


Improving future transformations


Findings from reviewed cases are fed back into the development process.

They can become additional training examples, updated detection rules, or regression tests that help prevent the same errors in future processing runs.


2.3 What reviewers actually inspect


Reviewers compare original and transformed artifacts and classify different types of problems.


These include:


  • Exposed PII that should have been transformed

  • Unnecessary transformations of information that should have remained unchanged

  • Incorrect PII classifications

  • Fragmented replacements where one identity has been assigned multiple synthetic identities

  • Identity collisions where different people have been merged

  • Inconsistent or unnatural replacements

  • Structural and presentation problems


Automated validation runs alongside human review.


It checks file integrity, structural preservation, expected output generation, and replacement consistency.


For real enterprise datasets, exhaustive manual inspection is generally impractical. Instead, the evaluation begins with the complete input inventory and selects representative samples for review.


Sampling can take into account file type, modality, PII density, model confidence, and known difficult cases.


Importantly, samples are selected from the original input inventory rather than only from successfully transformed files. This prevents failed or missing transformations from being excluded from the evaluation.

3. System Architecture

The overall architecture connects enterprise data ingestion, identity resolution, transformation, reconstruction, and quality control.


The process can be understood as the following sequence:


  1. Input: Enterprise datasets are received from approved sources.

  2. Security and configuration: Processing requirements, access controls, and transformation policies are applied.

  3. Inventory: Files are identified, categorized, and prepared for processing.

  4. Detection: Specialized models identify potentially sensitive information.

  5. Entity resolution: Related references are connected to their underlying identities.

  6. AI-assisted review: Difficult or uncertain cases are examined and corrected.

  7. Identity generation: Synthetic identity bundles are created for protected entities.

  8. Transformation: Original identifying values are replaced consistently across relevant artifacts.

  9. Reconstruction: Processed segments are assembled into usable files.

  10. Validation: Privacy, consistency, structure, and processing coverage are checked.

  11. Delivery: Transformed datasets and supporting quality reports are prepared.


Security, metadata management, identity resolution, and monitoring operate throughout the pipeline rather than being isolated to one stage.


The result is a transformed dataset that retains its useful business context without retaining the original protected identities.

4. Measuring Transformation Quality

4.1 Why detection accuracy is not enough


Traditional PII evaluation typically focuses on three metrics:

  • Precision: How many detected values were actually PII?

  • Recall: How much of the existing PII was successfully detected?

  • F1: A combined measure of precision and recall.


These metrics are useful for evaluating detection systems, but they do not measure the quality of the final transformed dataset.


A system might detect PII accurately while still producing a poor result.

For example:


  • It might replace the same employee with different synthetic identities across multiple files.

  • It might unnecessarily change information that should have remained untouched.

  • It might damage the structure of the original documents.

  • It might fail to process certain files entirely.


To measure the complete transformation process, we introduce the Transformation Quality Index (TQI).


TQI evaluates five areas: Privacy, Utility, Fidelity, Coverage, and Coherence.


5.2 The five evaluation pillars


Privacy


Privacy measures how much real identifying information remains exposed after transformation.


Not all exposed information carries the same risk.


For example, leaving a government identifier or bank account number exposed is generally more serious than leaving a lower-risk date or organization reference unchanged.


The evaluation therefore assigns different severity weights to different types of exposed information.


The score measures the proportion of weighted sensitive instances that were successfully protected.


Utility


Utility measures whether information that should have remained unchanged was unnecessarily transformed.


For example, replacing an employee's name is appropriate, but changing a product name, internal process, or technical identifier that contains no protected information may reduce the dataset's usefulness.


The evaluation considers how much useful information is lost through unnecessary transformations.


Changes that remove important business meaning receive a greater penalty.


Fidelity


Fidelity measures whether the transformed files remain structurally intact and usable.

Depending on the format, checks can include:


  • Number of rows and columns

  • Spreadsheet formulas and sheet names

  • Document page counts

  • Subtitle timing

  • Archive contents

  • Content outside the intended transformation regions


A file must not only contain the correct replacements. It must also remain a usable version of the original artifact.


Coverage


Coverage measures whether all expected inputs were actually processed.

The evaluation uses the original input inventory as its reference.

If a file fails, times out, or is rejected, it still counts toward the expected processing set.

This prevents systems from improving their results by silently excluding difficult files.


Coherence


Coherence measures whether the transformed dataset still makes sense as one connected collection of information.


It includes contextual consistency, presentation, and identity linkage.


Two important measures are used to evaluate identity linkage:


Linkage Recall: Measures whether references belonging to the same original entity remain connected to the same synthetic identity.


Linkage Precision: Measures whether references belonging to different original entities remain separate.


These are combined into Linkage F1.


This distinction matters because a dataset can look natural while containing fragmented identities, or maintain consistent identities while introducing incorrect connections.

Both problems need to be measured.


5.3 Calculating the Transformation Quality Index


The five evaluation pillars are combined into one overall score using a weighted geometric mean.


The formula is:

TQI = 100 × P^w1 × C^w2 × F^w3 × U^w4 × L^w5


Where:

  • P = Privacy

  • C = Coverage

  • F = Fidelity

  • U = Utility

  • L = Linkage quality

  • w = The assigned weight for each pillar


The weights can be adjusted according to the requirements and risk profile of the dataset.

Using a geometric mean prevents a particularly strong score in one area from completely compensating for a serious weakness in another.


The individual pillar scores, evaluation weights, sampling methodology, and reviewer criteria should always be reported alongside the final TQI.

5. Enterprise De-Identification Benchmark

We created a synthetic enterprise dataset to test PII detection, identity consistency, and information preservation.


It represents a fictional company with 600 employees and 420 customers across HR, Finance, Technology, Logs, and Customers. It includes realistic PII, misspellings, unusual formatting, and decoy information that should not be transformed.


We compared NVIDIA NeMo Anonymizer with flow-transform 1.0, both with and without agentic review.


5.1 Benchmark Results


With agentic review, flow-transform achieved a TQI of 88.9, compared with 84.1 without review and 74.9 for NeMo.

Its detection F1 reached 0.829, compared with 0.758 and 0.678, respectively. The decoy transformation rate also fell to 40%, reducing unnecessary changes to non-sensitive information.


5.2 Identity Consistency


We measured whether references to the same entity received consistent synthetic identities.

Flow-transform achieved a linkage F1 of 0.974. Across 730,034 resolved occurrences, 99.68% were collision-free.

6. Public Benchmark Evaluations

We also tested PII detection against public datasets.


On Tonic PrivacyBench, flow-transform achieved 96.00% F1, compared with 94.5% for Tonic Textual. Exact-span F1 reached 93.40%.


On Nemotron-PII, it achieved 88.33% F1, compared with 87.84% for NeMo. On AI4Privacy, it achieved 83.74%, compared with 73.08%.

7. Limitations and Next Steps

Current testing focuses on synthetic, structured data. Further work is needed to support complex file formats, images, audio, and video.


Other priorities include improving identity resolution, reducing collisions, preserving file structure, and validating results through human review.


Future evaluations will also compare how models perform when trained on original, redacted, synthetic, and identity-preserving transformed datasets.

Conclusion

Enterprise de-identification must protect identities without destroying the relationships and structure that make data useful.


Flow-transform combines PII detection, identity resolution, and synthetic replacement to achieve this. On the enterprise benchmark, it reached a TQI of 88.9 with agentic review.
